All 26 metro stations in Rome, mapped.

Twenty-six metro stations in NUM's directory of Rome, with the lines each one serves — and the short answer to why a city this size has only three lines and you will still be walking.

Where the concierge points people first

  • Metro Termini (MA, MB) — The only place lines A and B meet, which makes it unavoidable rather than good. Allow ten minutes to change here, more with a suitcase, and keep your bag in front of you on the platform — this is the one station in the city where that advice is not paranoia. (41.9008, 12.5019)
  • Metro San Giovanni (MA, MC) — Where line A meets line C, and worth arriving early for even if you are only changing. The line C platforms were built as a walk back through time — the deeper you go, the older the finds in the cases beside you, ending in a layer from before the city. (41.8855, 12.5097)
  • Metro Colosseo (MB, MC) — Opened as a line C interchange on 16 December 2025, after eighteen years of digging. You come up the stairs and the Colosseum is simply there, across the road, at a scale no photograph has prepared you for. Line B side is the old station and unremarkable; the new side is the one to look at. (41.8912, 12.4915)
  • Metro Porta Metronia (MC) — The other station that opened on 16 December 2025, one stop from the Colosseum and almost empty by comparison. Use it when Colosseo is a crush — it is a ten-minute walk to the same place, through the Celio rather than through the crowd. (41.8830, 12.5029)
  • Metro Circo Massimo (MB) — Comes out at the open end of the Circus Maximus, which is a free, unfenced, unticketed Roman monument you can simply walk onto. The best cheap hour in the city centre, and the Aventine orange garden is up the hill behind it. (41.8834, 12.4882)
  • Metro Cipro (MA) — The stop for the Vatican Museums, and the reason to know it is that the obvious one — Ottaviano — is where the queue touts wait. Cipro is one stop further and puts you nearer the entrance. (41.9076, 12.4474)

Rome has three metro lines because the ground is full of Rome

Rome has three metro lines. Paris has sixteen. The reason is not money or will: it is that you cannot dig anywhere in this city without hitting the city. Line C started archaeological investigation in August 2006 and did not finish until December 2025 — eighteen years for one line. A planned station at Largo di Torre Argentina was cancelled outright because the remains underneath turned out to be more extensive than expected, and the 2009 dig at Piazza Venezia ran into what has been identified as the Athenaeum of Hadrian. What this means on the day you are here is simple and worth planning around: the metro does not serve the historic centre. There is no stop at the Pantheon, none at Piazza Navona, none at Campo de' Fiori, none at Trevi. You will walk, or you will take a bus. Budget for that rather than discovering it at the top of the escalator.
